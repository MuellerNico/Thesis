\section{Numerical Tests}
\label{sec:numerical_tests}
In this chapter we present the results produced by SPH-EXA on a suite of standard MHD tests. With the first seven test cases we verify that our implementation produces results comparable to the reference codes \cite{WissingShen2020, Price2018}. We quantify accuracy and other relevant metrics such as the mean divergence error \eqref{eq:divb_error} and magnetic energy loss through artificial resistivity (resistive heating). Finally, in §\ref{sec:mhd_turb}, we apply a magnetic field to subsonic turbulence in a high-resolution simulation.

All tests are set up with glass-like initial conditions using the template glass blocks from SPH-EXA's public dataset \cite{Keller2023}. This is to be kept in mind when comparing SPH-EXA to codes using lattice-based initial conditions. The glass structure necessarily comes with some jittering due to particle reordering, resulting in a small but noticeable velocity noise floor. This will negatively impact certain tests which otherwise have almost perfectly uniform flow when using a lattice (e.g. the MHD advection loop §\ref{sec:MHD_loop}). 

For some MHD tests we did create initial conditions using both a regular grid and a face-centered cubic (FCC) grid. However, in our runs we see the lattices quickly melting into glass after only a few time steps. This is likely due to the kernel design choices of SPH-EXA, namely using a constant number \texttt{ng0} for the target number of neighbors combined with the sinc6 kernel (§\ref{sec:kernel_approximation}). A different kernel implementation should be able to keep a lattice structure and therefore uniform flow intact in SPH-EXA, but this is out of scope for this project.

Considering the presence of this velocity noise, we also expect the divergence error of most tests to be slightly larger than the corresponding references. Velocity noise enters the divergence error as one of the dominant source through the discrete induction equation, which is built from the velocity gradient tensor \eqref{eq:induction_code}. 

Whenever we refer to a resolution (e.g. $n_x = 250$) of a simulation using glass blocks, we refer to the resolution along the axes of the original grid with which the glass was generated. I.e. our glass block \texttt{50c.h} (read "50 cubed") was created from a $50 \times 50 \times 50$ lattice. The total number of particle remains the same, but they do not remain well ordered in the axis direction. 

The test setups follow those also used in Wissing \& Shen (2020) \cite{WissingShen2020} which in turn are based on Price et al. (2018) \cite{Price2018}. Differences are always made explicit. 

\paragraph{Default code parameters}
If not explicitly written otherwise, the test setups use the following default parameters. We use the sinc6 smoothing kernel with \texttt{ng0=150} desired and \texttt{ngmax=200} maximum neighbors per particle. AV uses AVSLR with constant $\alpha = 1.0$ (see §\ref{sec:slr}). And so does AR with ARSLR and $\alpha_B=1.0$. Magnetic constant $\mu_0 = 1$. We use an adiabatic EOS (equation of state) with $\gamma = 5/3$. Artificial conductivity (AC) is disabled as described in §\ref{sec:ac}. \todo[inline]{cleaning terms, other dissipation?}

\paragraph{Naming of AR schemes}
For simplicity we label the baseline scheme, using constant $\alpha_B = 0.5$, in our figures as \texttt{a05}. The schemes employing reconstruction as introduced in §\ref{sec:slr} are labelled \texttt{SLR}, \texttt{SLRB} and \texttt{SLRB2} respectively. All AR schemes use the self-switching $v_{sig,B}$ as defined in \eqref{eq:vsigB}.

\paragraph{Plotting Method}
\label{sec:plotting_method}
The two-dimensional slices plotted in the following sections are created with the following method: A particle $i$ is only selected if its distance to the slice plane is smaller than $2h_i$, i.e. the plane actually falls inside its kernel support. Then the selected particles are interpolated onto a regular grid of $512 \times 512$ pixels. Pixel values are calculated with the normalized SPH sum $ {\sum f_i W(r_{ip}/ h_i)}/{\sum W(r_{ip}/h_i)}$, where $W$ is the cubic spline kernel, $h_i$ the particle's smoothing length, and $r_{ip}=|r_i-r_p|$ the full 3D distance between the particle and the pixels center (including out-of-plane offset). This way of selection and interpolation tries to stay as close as possible to the actual physical representation.

\subsection{Alfvén Wave}

We perform the traveling circularized polarized (CP) Alfvén wave test \cite{Toth2000} which provides an analytical solution to the ideal MHD equations. Because the magnetic pressure is uniform, the wave is expected to remain the same after each period. It is therefore the most straight-forward correctness and accuracy test for our implementation. Deviation from the analytical solution over time is a good measurement of numerical dissipation and dispersion. 

The 3D setup follows the one in used in PHANTOM \cite{Price2018} and GASOLINE2 \cite{WissingShen2020}: A periodic box of size $L\times L/2 \times L/2$ with $L=3$ with the waves traveling at a $30^\circ$ angle to the $x$ axis get a true multidimensional test of the full 3D operators. The rotated parallel and transverse velocities and magnetic fields are: 

$ B_1 = 1, v_1 = 0\\
B_2 = v_1 = 0.1 \sin(2\pi x_1/\lambda), \\
B_3 = v_2 = 0.1 \cos(2\pi x_1/\lambda), $

where $x_1$ is the rotated direction of propagation and $\lambda=1$ is the wavelength. We use density $\rho = 1$ and uniform pressure $P=0.1$. Figure \ref{fig:alfven} shows the results after $t=5$ periods using $[n_x,n_y,n_z]=[120,60,60]$. We also plot the L1-error with increasing resolution and observe second-order convergence as expected. As per default parameters, all dissipation mechanisms (bar conductivity) are active.

\paragraph{Noisy Alfvén Wave}
We present a slight modification of the Alfvén wave test, not present in the reference literature, specifically to evaluate the performance of ARSLR. Additionally to the classic setup defined above, we add positionally-seeded white-noise perturbation to the magnetic field $B$ and velocity field $v$ in the initial conditions. With this we aim to observe how different dissipation schemes tackle incoherent noise (unresolved structure) versus the coherent wave (resolved structure). As described in §\ref{sec:slr}, the hope for ARSLR is to use full dissipation on the former while being able to linearly reconstruct and thus preserve the latter. 

To quantify this we perform a least-squares fit of the mode to both transverse components $B_2$ and $B_3$, and decompose the error into three errors: 
\begin{samepage}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Amplitude bleed: The ratio $A/A_0$ of the fitted mode amplitude $A$ to the initial $A_0=0.1$ to isolate resolved structure dissipation. 
    \item Phase error: The fitted phase minus the analytic phase to isolate dispersion. 
    \item $\delta B_n$: RMS of the mode subtracted transverse field to measure leftover noise perturbation.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}

Figure \ref{fig:alfven_noise} analyzes the results and compares different resistivity schemes. While both schemes perform very well, ARSLR wins out in both conserving the resolved structure (amplitude) and in dissipating unresolved, uncorrelated noise.

\subsection{Brio-Wu shocktube}
\label{sec:brio_wu}
The Brio-Wu shocktube is a simple extension of the Sod shocktube to MHD by applying an additional magnetic field on top of the usual setup. It is designed to test how well SPMHD codes handle different shocks and discontinuities. In the context of resistivity schemes, it is our primary test of this verification suite to ensure that shocks are not under-dissipated (i.e. AR is not applied too weakly) which would result in overshoot and post-shock oscillations (see §\ref{sec:ar}). 

While there is no analytical solution to the Brio-Wu shocktube as there is for the Alfvén wave test, we can use a reference solution calculated with a high-resolution grid code. 

We initialize a 3D thin periodic box of $[n_x,n_y,n_z] = [960, 60, 60]$ particles on the left side $[-2,0]$ and $[n_x,n_y,n_z] = [480, 30, 30]$ particles on the right side $[0,2]$ to get our desired density contrast of ratio $\rho_{left}/\rho_{right} = 8$. Field variables are initialized discontinuously as: 

$x\leq 0: \; (\rho_L, P_L, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_x, B_y, B_z) = (1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0.75, 1, 0),\\
x > 0: \; (\rho_R, P_R, v_x, v_y, v_z, B_x, B_y, B_z) = (0.125, 0.1, 0, 0, 0, 0.75, -1, 0).$

The adiabatic index is $\gamma = 2$. We use constant AV with $\alpha = 1.0$\todo{double check value}. This choice is made because we want to specifically evaluate the performance of the MHD implementation and not the hydro components. Constant AV improves the solution shape by reducing the amount of oscillations and overshoots compared to AVSLR or AVSW. This way we can better isolate and compare the effects of AR schemes. 

Figure \ref{fig:brio-wu} shows the results after $t=0.2$. Particles within the active middle region $x\in [-1,1]$ are projected onto the $x$-axis. Additionally to the L1 error, each panel shows an \texttt{osc}-error, intended to measure post-shock ringing. It is defined as the maximum deviation from the reference solution $max(|q-q_{ref}|)$, but restricted to flat plateau areas of the solution where $|\frac{dq_{ref}}{dx}|<0.2$. Additionally, these intervals are trimmed by $0.03$ off each end to exclude smeared shock-fronts. This metric is to be viewed with care as it is dominated by outliers. Both errors are calculated only over the window visible in Figure \ref{fig:brio_wu}.

SLR wins out slightly over the baseline in most panels. Notably, showing slightly \textit{less} post-shock oscillation in $B_y$ while simultaneously also showing s\textit{less} resistive heating $\dot{u}_{diss}$\eqref{eq:res_heating}.

The results can be compared to Fig. 4 in Wissing \& Shen (2020)\cite{WissingShen2020}, Fig. 28/29 in Price et al. (2018)\cite{Price2018} and Fig. 2 Hopkins \& Raives (2015)\cite{Hopkins2015}. Divergence errors show a higher floor, even before the shock-front, likely due to the velocity noise floor discussed at the beginning of §\ref{sec:numerical_tests}. 

Some hydro artifacts remain despite constant AV, such as the pressure spike around $x=0.1$ which also appears in a pure hydrodynamic run with magnetic forces disabled.

\subsection{Magnetized Sedov blast}

In this magnetized blast wave we have a central, over-pressurized region which then expands preferentially along magnetic field lines instead of uniformly. This lets us i.a. test energy conservation under extreme pressure. 

A 3D square box of length $L=1$ is initialized with uniform density and resolution $N=250^3$. The inner region (radius $R=0.125$) is over-pressurized with $P_{in}=100$ while $P_{out}=1$). The initial magnetic field is set to $B = [10/ \sqrt{2}, 0, 10/\sqrt{2}]$, giving plasma beta $\beta_{in}=2$ and $\beta_{out}=0.02$. 

Figure \ref{fig:sedov} shows the results after $t=0.02$. Compared to Fig. 8 in Wissing \& Shen (2020)\cite{WissingShen2020}, the bean-like shape of our blast wave appears a bit narrower with a slightly thicker shock front. The precise internal structure is hard to compare as their Fig 8. is a bit blurry (especially apparent when comparing the pressure panel). We used same per-particle-h and kernel interpolation plotting method as described in the beginning of §\ref{sec:numerical_tests}. But overall we observe similar structure. We also clearly see the expected double-shock in thermal pressure and the outer ring in the other three panels. 

Figure \ref{fig:sedov_metrics} shows that the conservation of total energy and the mean divergence error both improve with increasing resolution. Compared to the reported number of $\langle \varepsilon_{\text{divB}}\rangle=10^{-5}$ in Wissing \& Shen (2020) \cite{WissingShen2020}, these results are still one order of magnitude worse.

\subsection{Current Loop Advection}
\label{sec:MHD_loop}

A weak magnetic loop is advected by a constant velocity field. The magnetic field is chosen small enough to be dynamically unimportant. In theory, the loop is thus expected to simply be moved along the velocity field and maintain shape throughout. This test is therefore great for determining numerical diffusion of the magnetic field. 

A thin periodic box is initialized with length $L=[2,1,2\frac{\sqrt{6}}{n_x}]$, velocity $v=[2,1,\frac{15}{n_x}]$, uniform pressure $P=1$, uniform density $\rho = 1$ and resolution $[n_x, n_y, n_z] = [120, 60, 30]$. The magnetic field inside the loop of radius $R_0=0.3$ is defined as ${B_0} = \frac{A_0}{r}(y, -x, 0)$ with $A_0=10^{-3}$. Outside the loop the magnetic field is zero. The loop is advected for $t=20$ periods. 

This test is especially susceptible to the velocity noise floor discussed in the beginning of §\ref{sec:numerical_tests}. The flow needed to conduct this test adequately needs to be uniform and almost perfectly smooth, which SPH-EXA with its glass-setup and kernel choice fails to deliver. Thus, the results are hard to compare against those in the reference papers using lattice initial conditions. 

Figure \ref{fig:loop_av_comp} compares four different runs, all using the same ARSLR scheme but combined with different AV schemes. It is clear that constant AV performs best at maintaining the loops shape, followed by AVSLR and lastly AVSW. This is a direct consequence of how well each scheme smoothes out the velocity field, specifically sub-resolution shear-noise. 

Constant AV with $\alpha = 1$ is constantly tackling any kind of noise based on the approach velocity $\omega_{ab}$ of two particles (see eq. \eqref{eq:av_Pi}. AVSLR subtracts the linear part of the approach velocity fully due to the Balsara-modulator which is designed to specifically detect shear flow and reduce the impact of AV in those cases. Meanwhile AVSW reduces $\alpha$ to $\alpha_{min}$ because switches only activate when there is compression, i.e. $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}<0$ \cite{MorrisMonaghan1997}. 

Remaining velocity noise leads to two effects via its small scale unphysical transverse flow: $E_{mag}$ injection by stretching the field (small scale dynamo via the induction equation \eqref{eq:induction}), and increased dissipation via the signal velocity $v_{\mathrm{sig},B} = |\mathbf{v}_{ab} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab}|$ \eqref{eq:vsigB} used for resistivity \eqref{eq:ar_dB}. Two large noise driven terms which deteriorate the structure and violate conservation of magnetic energy in the direction of whichever term dominates. 

With this knowledge in mind, Figure \ref{fig:loop_ar_comp} uses constant AV to minimize the impact of velocity noise and then compares different AR schemes. Both a05 and SLR maintain the loop equally well, with the latter maybe showing marginally smoother interior and edges. A run using no magnetic dissipation ($\alpha_B=0$) is shown as reference. Figure \ref{fig:loop_metrics} then analyzes resistive heating and magnetic energy evolution of all three configurations with SLR winning out slightly over a05, showing $28\%$ less cumulative heating up to $t=20$.

\subsection{Subsonic MHD Turbulence}
\label{sec:mhd_turb}

Application. DivB error + resistive heating. $1600^3$ particles (more than 4 billion). 

Large-scale mean field, mildly super-alfvenic. $B=B_0\hat{z}$ with $B_0 = 0.15$. 
gamma=1.001

\begin{equation}
    \beta = \frac{P_{gas}}{P_{mag}} = \frac{\rho c^2}{B_0^2 / 2 \mu_0} \Rightarrow B_0 = \sqrt{2 \rho c^2 / \beta}
\end{equation}

Plasmabeta $\beta$. Sound speed $c=1$, Alfvén speed $v_A = \frac{|B|}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \rho}}$. $v_{RMS} = 0.3$ (subsonic). Sonic mach number $M = \frac{v_{RMS}}{c} = 0.3$. Alfvenic mach number $M_A = \frac{v_{RMS}}{v_A}=2$ (>1, super-alfvenic, turbulence dominates).
