\subsection{Classic SPH Theory}
At its core, the SPH method solves the Euler equations by evolving an ensemble of macro-particles. The mean inter-particle spacing is represented by the smoothing length $h$. The particles hold local values for physical quantities, they are our interpolation-points. Between the particles, these are evaluated through summation using a smoothing kernel $W(r_{ab},h)$ with characteristic length $h$. Derivatives are calculated without of finite differences by using the known analytic form of the kernel derivative, i.e. we use the exact derivative of the approximated function.

\subsection{ISPH}

ISPH: (IAD/GVE) discretization. Perfect conservation but not perfect linear interpolation. Partition of unity (makes interpolation nearly exact), strong when disordered particles or near discontinuities. Accounts for uneven sampling in space (similar to weighted least squares). 

ISPH gradient operator

\subsection{Artificial Resistivity} 
Discontinuities, stability, motivation. 

Finite particles cannot represent a true discontinuity which would be true physical solution for a shock or sharp gradient. So energy in the sharp gradient has nowhere to go, can't propagate away cleanly. Instead bounces back as high-freq oscillations -> Gibbs-like ringing artifacts near shocks. Without dissipation those oscillations don't decay but grow and blow up solution. Just need to give energy somewhere to go, like a drain or lowpass filter at resolution scale. Rather dump energy locally than corrupt rest of solution. Tangential and rotational discontinuities require AR (no compression there. vsig=0, compression is AVs job). fast and slow shock via AV? Contact discontinuity neither (ideally).

\subsubsection{SLRV, SLRV2, SLRC (failed attempts) - notes to be rewritten}
Idea: replace the $F_{ab}$ limiter with a vector van Leer using the full $\mathbf{J}_{B_a}\mathbf{x}_{ab}^T$ instead of just its longitudinal (twice-$\mathbf{x}_{ab}$-contracted) projection, since B-discontinuities are mostly transverse (B\_normal continuous across MHD fronts, so $F_{ab}$ compares the least informative component). Also clamp the correction (minmod-style) so it can never exceed or flip the sign of $B_{ab}$, dropping $\kappa$ and the Tricco-Price modulator entirely (SLRV). Two follow-up ablations: same vector limiter without the clamp (SLRV2), and a per-component minmod between the two one-sided candidates $-\mathbf{J}_{B_a}\mathbf{x}_{ab}^T$, $-\mathbf{J}_{B_b}\mathbf{x}_{ab}^T$ instead of a shared confidence number (SLRC).

Tested on Brio-Wu and the (optionally noise-seeded) Alfvén wave. All three land in the same place, clearly worse than plain SLR: ring at the shock fronts on Brio-Wu, worse than the constant-$\alpha_B$ baseline; on the noisy wave they preserve the wave amplitude better than SLR (less bleed) but barely touch the injected noise (residual power $P_n$ stays $\sim$4$\times$ SLR's -- worse than doing nothing scheme-specific at all).

The van Leer limiter is designed to approach zero whenever the local linear estimator stops being accurate, discontinuity or noise. $F_{ab}$ tests consistency along a single fixed direction ($\mathbf{x}_{ab}$, contracted twice), so local disagreement has nowhere to hide. The vector-based variants compare the full 3-vector $\mathbf{J}_B\mathbf{x}_{ab}^T$ on each side; noise that disrupts one component can still be outvoted/diluted by the other two agreeing (blend) or by both sides' per-component estimates rarely disagreeing in sign to begin with (minmod), since $\mathcal{O}(150)$-neighbour gradient smoothing suppresses noise similarly on all 3 components at once. Same designed sensitivity, just diluted by aggregating over more directions/components than the scalar test does.

Conclusion: plain SLR ($F_{ab}$, no Balsara-like modulation) is still the best performer on both fronts and noise; SLRB is statistically indistinguishable from it. Fixing the vector family properly would mean not diluting the agreement test across components while still using more of the gradient tensor, not a better limiter shape on top of the current one -- bigger rework, not pursued further given the time budget. Branch closed.

\textbf{Open concern with plain SLR (not yet tested):} $F_{ab}$'s testing scope is narrow. For AV on velocity, the dissipated quantity $\omega_{ab}=\mathbf{v}_{ab}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{ab}$ is itself a scalar along the pair direction by construction (Balsara: exclude transverse/shear motion from dissipation on purpose), so the test and the dissipated content live in the same 1D subspace -- no mismatch possible. For AR, the dissipated content is the full $B_{ab}$ vector, and MHD discontinuities (rotational fronts, current sheets) are mostly transverse -- so a single-direction test gating a 3-component correction is a new mismatch introduced specifically by porting SLR from velocity to the induction equation. In Brio-Wu this doesn't bite because the field only varies along $x$, so any pair's $\mathbf{x}_{ab}$ samples the true (x-only) gradient regardless of which B-component carries the jump. It's untested whether this holds for genuinely oblique/multi-dimensional fronts (Orszag-Tang, rotor, turbulence, reconnection): a pair whose separation happens to sit near-perpendicular to the true local gradient direction gets an ill-conditioned, essentially arbitrary $F_{ab}$ regardless of how large the real transverse jump is -- plausibly giving orientation-dependent, patchy shock capture across a single front rather than the clean, direction-independent behaviour seen in the aligned 1D case.

\emph{Outlook:} fixing this without reintroducing the failures encountered means using more of the gradient tensor's transverse information \emph{without} diluting the single-direction sensitivity that makes $F_{ab}$ work. One direction worth trying: keep the test single-direction and well-conditioned \emph{per pair} (so each comparison stays as sensitive as $F_{ab}$ is now), but probe a front from several different pair orientations at once -- e.g.\ require agreement across multiple neighbours with different $\mathbf{x}_{ab}$ directions before trusting reconstruction, rather than blending/aggregating a single pair's full tensor. That's different from what SLRV/SLRV2/SLRC did (aggregate 3 components \emph{within} one pair) -- it aggregates \emph{across pairs}, each of which stays a narrow, sensitive, single-direction test, so an unlucky orientation for one neighbour doesn't matter as long as others aren't also unlucky.
