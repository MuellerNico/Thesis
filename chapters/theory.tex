\section{Theory and numerical method}
\label{sec:theory}

This chapter establishes the mathematical and numerical foundation for the SPMHD implementation within SPH-EXA. We briefly introduce the core concepts of SPH and cover the integral approach to derivatives (IAD) and generalized volume elements that characterize the ISPH formulation used in SPH-EXA. We extend this methodology to ideal MHD, presenting the necessary dissipation mechanisms, along with a divergence-cleaning scheme for numerical stability.

The equations presented here reflect the actual implementation in three spatial dimensions in the code, with explicit mention of where our discretization choices diverge from the reference works. 

\subsection{Smoothed particle hydrodynamics}
\label{sec:sph}

SPH is a Lagrangian, mesh-free approach for solving equations of fluid dynamics \cite{Rosswog2009}. The domain is represented by an ensemble of macro-particles that carry local values of physical quantities (e.g. density, velocity, internal energy, etc.) serving as discrete interpolation points. The fluid is integrated in time by evolving these macro-particles. Rather than using finite differences on a grid, spatial fields are approximated through convolution with a smoothing kernel. %, and derivatives are obtained analytically from the known kernel form.

% \subsubsection*{Kernel approximation}
\label{sec:kernel_approximation}
The kernel approximation of a function $f$ at position $\mathbf{r}$ is given by \cite{Rosswog2009}
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{f}_h(\mathbf{r}) = \int f(\mathbf{r}') \, W(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}', h) \, d^3 r'\,,
    \label{eq:kernel_approx}
\end{equation}
where $W$ is the smoothing kernel with smoothing length $h$ determining spatial resolution. The kernel must satisfy normalization ($\int W(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}', h) \, d^3 r' = 1$), delta convergence ($\lim_{h \to 0} W(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}', h) = \delta(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}')$), have compact support and is chosen to be radially symmetric to ensure exact angular-momentum conservation \cite{Rosswog2009}. SPH-EXA uses the sinc-kernel family introduced with SPHYNX \cite{Cabezon2017}, defined through powers of the cardinal sine function. This approximation reproduces constant and linear functions exactly and is second-order accurate in $h$.
% \begin{equation}
%     \tilde{f}_h(\mathbf{r}) = f(\mathbf{r}) + \mathcal{O}(h^2)\,.
%     \label{eq:kernel_accuracy}
% \end{equation}

% \subsubsection*{Particle approximation}

We can discretize the integral approximation \eqref{eq:kernel_approx} by replacing $\rho(\mathbf{r}')\,d^3r'$ with the particle mass $m_b$ to get the SPH interpolation sum \cite{Rosswog2009}
\begin{equation}
    f(\mathbf{r}_a) \approx \sum_b \frac{m_b}{\rho_b}\, f_b \, W_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:sph_interp}
\end{equation}
where $W_{ab} \equiv W(|\mathbf{r}_a - \mathbf{r}_b|, h_a)$ and the sum extends over all neighbors $b$ within the kernel support. The ratio $m_b/\rho_b$ serves as the volume element for particle $b$. Setting $f = \rho$ in \eqref{eq:sph_interp} gives the standard SPH density estimate
\begin{equation}
    \rho_a = \sum_b m_b \, W_{ab}(h_a)\,.
    \label{eq:density_sum}
\end{equation}
Particle masses remain fixed, so mass conservation is exact by construction.

% \subsubsection*{Gradient evaluation}

A key feature of SPH is that spatial derivatives are computed without finite-differences, but by differentiating \eqref{eq:sph_interp} analytically, using the known analytic form of the kernel gradient \cite{Rosswog2009}:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla f(\mathbf{r}_a) \approx \sum_b \frac{m_b}{\rho_b} \, f_b \, \nabla_a W_{ab}\,.
    \label{eq:sph_grad}
\end{equation}
Here $\nabla_a W_{ab}$ represents a radial vector from $b$ to $a$ that vanishes when $a = b$. This approach means gradient accuracy depends on how well the kernel reproduces the underlying function. In practice, the naive sum \eqref{eq:sph_grad} only achieves zeroth-order consistency (it does not reproduce the gradient of a linear field exactly on disordered particle distributions), motivating the IAD correction discussed in \S\ref{sec:iad}.

Second derivatives employ the Brookshaw form \cite{Brookshaw1985}, which avoids noise amplification from double gradients and appears in artificial resistivity (\S\ref{sec:ar}):
\begin{equation}
    (\nabla^2 f)_a = 2 \sum_b \frac{m_b}{\rho_b} (f_a - f_b) \, \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab} \cdot \nabla_a W_{ab}}{|\mathbf{r}_{ab}|}\,.
    \label{eq:brookshaw}
\end{equation}

% \subsubsection*{The Euler equations in SPH}

SPH solves the Lagrangian form of the Euler equations. The continuity equation (conservation of mass), the momentum equation (conservation of momentum) and the energy equation (conservation of energy) are: 
\begin{align}
    \frac{d\rho}{dt}   &= -\rho \, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}\,, \label{eq:euler_cont} \\
    \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} &= -\frac{\nabla P}{\rho}\,, \label{eq:euler_mom} \\
    \frac{du}{dt}       &= -\frac{P}{\rho} \, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}\,, \label{eq:euler_energy}
\end{align}
where $\rho$ is density, $\mathbf{v}$ velocity, $P$ pressure, and $u$ specific internal energy. Self-gravity and other body forces are omitted for clarity.

Direct discretization of \eqref{eq:euler_mom} using \eqref{eq:sph_grad} does not conserve momentum because the resulting pairwise forces are not antisymmetric \cite{Rosswog2009}. Therefore, the pressure gradient is usually rewritten using the product-rule identity%$\nabla P / \rho = \nabla(P/\rho^2) \cdot \rho + (P/\rho^2)\nabla\rho$
, yielding the symmetric form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v}_a}{dt} = -\sum_b m_b \left( \frac{P_a}{\rho_a^2} + \frac{P_b}{\rho_b^2} \right) \nabla_a W_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:sph_mom_vanilla}
\end{equation}
with conjugate energy equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{du_a}{dt} = \frac{P_a}{\rho_a^2} \sum_b m_b \, \mathbf{v}_{ab} \cdot \nabla_a W_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:sph_energy_vanilla}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_{ab} = \mathbf{v}_a - \mathbf{v}_b$. The symmetric appearance of pressure terms in indices $a$ and $b$ ensures Newton's third law holds pairwise.


% \subsubsection*{Variational derivation and grad-h correction}

Equations \eqref{eq:sph_mom_vanilla}--\eqref{eq:sph_energy_vanilla} can be derived from a discretized fluid Lagrangian via the Euler--Lagrange equations \cite{Rosswog2009}, guaranteeing conservation of linear and angular momentum and total energy by construction. When each particle uses its own smoothing length $h_a$, the variational derivation introduces the grad-h correction factor \cite{Rosswog2009}
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_a = 1 - \frac{\partial h_a}{\partial \rho_a} \sum_b m_b \frac{\partial W_{ab}(h_a)}{\partial h_a}\,,
    \label{eq:gradh_omega}
\end{equation}
and the self-consistent momentum equation becomes
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v}_a}{dt} = -\sum_b m_b \left( \frac{P_a}{\Omega_a \rho_a^2} \nabla_a W_{ab}(h_a) + \frac{P_b}{\Omega_b \rho_b^2} \nabla_a W_{ab}(h_b) \right)\,,
    \label{eq:sph_mom_gradh}
\end{equation}
where each term uses the kernel evaluated with the respective particle's smoothing length.


\subsection{The integral approach to derivatives and generalized volume elements}
\label{sec:iad}

The standard SPH gradient \eqref{eq:sph_grad} reproduces the gradient of a constant but not of a linear field on disordered particle distributions. The error is controlled by how well the kernel sum approximates the partition of unity $\sum_b V_b W_{ab} = 1$, which degrades near boundaries, density jumps, and general uneven particle sampling \cite{Cabezon2012}. The integral approach to derivatives (IAD) corrects this through renormalization of the gradient. One constructs a symmetric $3\times 3$ correction matrix $\boldsymbol{\tau}_a$ from local particle geometry, whose inverse $c_{ij,a} = (\boldsymbol{\tau}_a^{-1})_{ij}$ (precomputed once per timestep) defines the corrected gradient operator \cite{Cabezon2012, Cabezon2017}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{A}_{i,ab}(h_a) = \sum_{j} c_{ij,a} \, (x_{j,b} - x_{j,a}) \, W_{ab}(h_a)\,.
    \label{eq:iad_grad}
\end{equation}
This replaces the standard kernel gradient everywhere in the SPH equations:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial W_{ab}(h_a)}{\partial x_{i,a}} \longrightarrow \mathcal{A}_{i,ab}(h_a)\,.
    \label{eq:iad_replacement}
\end{equation}
Meaning, when we later adapt the SPMHD equations from reference codes for SPH-EXA, we perform exactly this substitution. By construction, $\mathcal{A}$ reproduces the gradient of any linear function exactly regardless of particle distribution, making the IAD scheme first-order consistent \cite{Cabezon2012}. % \subsubsection*{Generalized volume elements} 
The accuracy of SPH interpolation also depends on the satisfaction of the partition of unity. The standard choice for particle volumes $V_b = m_b/\rho_b$ can deviate significantly from unity near shocks and density discontinuities. Therefore, the generalized volume element (GVE) framework \cite{Cabezon2017, GarciaSenz2022} defines an auxiliary scalar $X_a$ and obtains the volume via
\begin{equation}
    V_a = \frac{X_a}{k_a}\,, \qquad k_a = \sum_b X_b \, W_{ab}(h_a)\,,
    \label{eq:gve}
\end{equation}
with density recovered as $\rho_a = m_a/V_a = k_a m_a/X_a$. The choice implemented in SPH-EXA is $X_a = m_a/\rho_a^0$, where $\rho_a^0 = \sum_b m_b W_{ab}$ is the standard summation density \cite{GarciaSenz2022}. This construction improves the accuracy of all gradient evaluations. \todo{difference first and 2nd paragraph} %represents a fixed-point iteration driving the partition-of-unity error toward zero, improving the accuracy of all gradient evaluations.

% \subsubsection*{The ISPH momentum and energy equations}

Combining the IAD gradient, GVE, and $\Omega_a$ grad-h correction within the variational framework of §\ref{sec:sph} yields the ISPH (``Integral SPH'') momentum equation \cite{GarciaSenz2022}:

\begin{equation}
    \ddot{x}^i_a = -\sum_b m_b \left[\frac{X_a^2 P_a}{\Omega_a m_a^2 k_a} \, \mathcal{A}_{i,ab}(h_a) + \frac{X_b^2 P_b}{\Omega_b m_b^2 k_b} \, \mathcal{A}_{i,ab}(h_b) \right]\,,
    \label{eq:isph_mom}
\end{equation}
with conjugate energy equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{du_a}{dt} = \frac{X_a^2 P_a}{\Omega_a m_a^2 k_a} \sum_b m_b \, v^i_{ab} \, \mathcal{A}_{i,ab}(h_a)\,.
    \label{eq:isph_energy}
\end{equation}
The structure mirrors the standard grad-h SPH equations \eqref{eq:sph_mom_gradh}, with the kernel gradients replaced by IAD operators and $1/\rho^2$ replaced by the GVE-derived $X^2/(m^2 k)$. When the partition of unity is exact ($k_a X_a = m_a$, i.e.\ $\rho_a = \rho_a^0$), \eqref{eq:isph_mom} reduces to \eqref{eq:sph_mom_gradh} with IAD gradients. %Conservation of linear and angular momentum remains exact and energy conservation is guaranteed through the variational derivation \cite{GarciaSenz2022}.

SPH-EXA additionally implements a smooth transition between the ISPH symmetrization in \eqref{eq:isph_mom} and a more diffusive form for large density contrasts, controlled by the Atwood number $\mathcal{A}_t = |\rho_a - \rho_b|/(\rho_a + \rho_b)$. Below a threshold $\mathcal{A}_{t,\min}$ the ISPH form applies; above $\mathcal{A}_{t,\max}$ the volume-element prefactors become symmetric ($X_a X_b$ in both terms), similar to the GDSPH discretization \cite{WissingShen2020}.

% The parameter $\sigma_{ab} \in [0,1]$ controls the symmetrization of the volume-element prefactors \cite{GarciaSenz2022}. At $\sigma = 0$ the pure ISPH form is recovered (prefactors $X_a^2$ and $X_b^2$), which reduces to \eqref{eq:sph_mom_gradh} with IAD gradients when the partition of unity is exact ($k_a X_a = m_a$). At $\sigma = 1$ the prefactors become symmetric ($X_a X_b$), recovering a form similar to the GDSPH discretization \cite{WissingShen2020}. In SPH-EXA, $\sigma$ is set per particle pair from the local Atwood number.% $\mathcal{A}_t = |\rho_a - \rho_b|/(\rho_a + \rho_b)$: below a threshold $\mathcal{A}_{t,\min}$, $\sigma = 0$ (ISPH); above $\mathcal{A}_{t,\max}$, $\sigma = 1$ (GDSPH-like); a linear ramp interpolates between the two. 
% This prevents the E0-error of the ISPH kernel normalization from generating spurious forces at strong density contrasts \cite{GarciaSenz2022}. %Conservation of linear and angular momentum remains exact; energy conservation follows from the variational derivation.

\subsection{Artificial viscosity}
\label{sec:av}

The Euler equations admit shock discontinuities that finite-resolution SPH particles cannot represent directly. Without additional dissipation, shocks produce unphysical post-shock oscillations. AV provides controlled, resolution-scale dissipation that converts kinetic energy of converging flow into heat, widening shocks over a few smoothing lengths \cite{Rosswog2009}. The standard Monaghan AV adds a pairwise viscous pressure $\Pi_{ab}$ to the momentum and energy equations, active only for approaching particles, %($\mathbf{v}_{ab} \cdot \mathbf{r}_{ab} < 0$). In the signal-velocity formulation used in SPH-EXA \cite{Rosswog2009},
\begin{equation}
    \Pi_{ab} = \frac{1}{\bar{\rho}_{ab}} \left[\frac{\alpha}{4}(\bar{c}_a + \bar{c}_b)\, w_{ab} + \beta \, w_{ab}^2 \right]\,,
    \label{eq:av_Pi}
\end{equation}
with $w_{ab} = \mathbf{v}_{ab} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab}$ the projected approach velocity, $\bar{c}$ the signal speed (sound speed in hydro, fast magnetosonic speed in MHD), a dimensionless coefficient $\alpha$, and $\beta = 2$. The linear term damps oscillations at the shock front; the quadratic term prevents particle interpenetration in strong shocks. 

Away from shocks, AV generates unwanted dissipation that damps physical shear flows and vortices. The Balsara limiter \cite{Balsara1995} tries to address this by distinguishing compression from shear flow via
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{B}_a = \frac{|\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}|_a}{|\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}|_a + |\nabla \times \mathbf{v}|_a + \epsilon_a}\,, \qquad \epsilon_a = 10^{-4}\,\frac{c_a}{h_a}\,,
    \label{eq:balsara}
\end{equation}
which approaches unity in convergent flow and zero in pure shear. We then multiply $\Pi_{ab}$ with the pair-averaged $\mathcal{B}_{ab}$. One can further reduce dissipation by treating the per-particle $\alpha$ as a control knob and use so-called heuristic switches to dynamically increase its value in compressions and reduce it in smooth flow \cite{MorrisMonaghan1997}. 

A more recent approach tries to get rid of heuristic switches and instead use slope-limited reconstruction to subtract the linear part of the velocity field from $\mathbf{v}_{ab}$ and thus reduce dissipation in resolved flow \cite{GarciaSenzCabezon2026}. 

\subsection{Ideal magnetohydrodynamics}
\label{sec:mhd}

Ideal magnetohydrodynamics extends the Euler equations with the Lorentz force and induction equation to describe perfectly conducting fluids threaded by magnetic fields $\mathbf{B}$.
% \subsubsection*{The Lorentz force}
Assuming $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}=0$, the contribution of the magnetic field to the acceleration is \cite{WissingShen2020}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}\bigg|_\mathrm{mag} = \frac{1}{\rho} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S}\,,
    \label{eq:lorentz}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{S}$ is the Maxwell stress tensor
\begin{equation}
    S^{ij} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left(B^i B^j - \frac{1}{2}\delta^{ij} B^2\right)\,.
    \label{eq:maxwell_stress}
\end{equation}
The isotropic part $-B^2/(2\mu_0)$ acts as a magnetic pressure, while the anisotropic part $B^i B^j/\mu_0$ produces tension along field lines. Combining \ref{eq:euler_mom} with \ref{eq:lorentz}, the MHD momentum equation is 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = \frac{-\nabla P}{\rho} + \frac{\nabla \cdot \mathbf{S}}{\rho}.
    \label{eq:mhd_momentum}
\end{equation}

% \subsubsection*{The induction equation}

For a perfectly conducting fluid, the magnetic field evolves according to the induction equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{B}}{dt} = (\mathbf{B} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{B}(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v})\,.
    \label{eq:induction}
\end{equation}
The first term stretches and bends field lines through shearing motion; the second amplifies the field in compressions.

% \subsubsection*{The divergence constraint}
\label{sec:divB_constraint}

Maxwell's equations require $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ at all times. In continuum MHD this is preserved automatically: if the initial field is divergence-free and the induction equation holds exactly, $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ remains zero. Numerically, however, discretization errors violate this constraint, producing unphysical magnetic monopoles that generate spurious forces and destabilize simulations. The normalized divergence error
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{\mathrm{divB}} = \frac{h\,|\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}|}{|\mathbf{B}|}
    \label{eq:divb_error}
\end{equation}
provides a dimensionless diagnostic. A well-controlled simulation maintains a mean divergence error $\langle \varepsilon_{divB}\rangle \lesssim 10^{-2}$ \cite{WissingShen2020, Price2018}. Controlling these errors is central to SPMHD and addressed via the divergence-cleaning scheme (§\ref{sec:divb_cleaning}).

\subsection{SPMHD discretization in SPH-EXA}
\label{sec:spmhd}

We now present the SPH discretization of MHD equations as implemented in SPH-EXA. The code builds on the ISPH framework of \S\ref{sec:iad} and draws on elements from both the PHANTOM code \cite{Price2018} and the MHD version of GASOLINE2 \cite{WissingShen2020}, adapting them to IAD gradients and generalized volume elements.

% \subsubsection*{Momentum equation with magnetic stress}

The magnetic contribution to the momentum equation uses the same ISPH structure as \eqref{eq:isph_mom}, with the Maxwell stress tensor \eqref{eq:maxwell_stress} entering alongside gas pressure:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dv^i_a}{dt}\bigg|_\mathrm{mag} = \sum_b m_b \left[ \frac{X_a^2}{\Omega_a m_a^2 k_a}\, S_a^{ij}\, \mathcal{A}_{j,ab}(h_a) + \frac{X_b^2}{\Omega_b m_b^2 k_b}\, S_b^{ij}\, \mathcal{A}_{j,ab}(h_b) \right]\,.
    \label{eq:spmhd_mom}
\end{equation}
This represents a Phantom-style $\rho^2$ symmetrization (each particle uses its own stress tensor and volume-element prefactor) adapted to the ISPH framework. %rather than the GDSPH form of Wissing \& Shen \cite{WissingShen2020} which uses $S_a^{ij} + S_b^{ij}$ with a single $1/(\rho_a \rho_b)$ prefactor \cite{Price2018}. 
The Atwood-number blending described in \S\ref{sec:iad} applies to both gas pressure and magnetic stress terms.

Rather than discretizing the induction equation \eqref{eq:induction} as a direct antisymmetric SPH sum, SPH-EXA first computes the full velocity-gradient tensor (Jacobian) 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial v^i}{\partial x^j}\bigg|_a = \frac{K}{h_a^3}\frac{1}{k_a \Omega_a} \sum_b V_b \,(v^i_b - v^i_a)\, \mathcal{A}_{j,ab}(h_a)
    \label{eq:vel_jacobian} 
\end{equation}
via the IAD gradient, then evaluates \eqref{eq:induction} component by component:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dB^x_a}{dt} = -B^x_a \left(\frac{\partial v^y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v^z}{\partial z}\right)_{\!a} + B^y_a \frac{\partial v^x}{\partial y}\bigg|_a + B^z_a \frac{\partial v^x}{\partial z}\bigg|_a\,,
    \label{eq:induction_code}
\end{equation} 
and analogously for the other components. This yields the same continuum operator as the direct sum but with a more accurate (IAD-based) gradient estimator.

% \subsubsection*{The fast magnetosonic signal speed}

In MHD, information propagates as sound, Alfvén, and magnetosonic waves. The relevant signal speed for shock capturing and time-stepping is the fast magnetosonic speed
\begin{equation}
    v_\mathrm{mhd} = \sqrt{c_s^2 + v_A^2}\,, \qquad v_A = \sqrt{\frac{B^2}{\mu_0 \rho}}\,,
    \label{eq:vmhd}
\end{equation}
where $c_s$ is the speed of sound and $v_A$ is the Alfvén speed \cite{WissingShen2020, Price2018}. SPH-EXA replaces the sound speed $c_s$ in the artificial viscosity signal velocity \eqref{eq:av_Pi} with $v_\mathrm{mhd}$, and uses the per-pair maximum signal speed for its CFL time-step constraint.

% \subsubsection*{Tensile instability correction}

When magnetic pressure exceeds gas pressure (plasmabeta $\beta = 2\mu_0 P / B^2 < 1$), tensile instability can occur due to divergence errors. Particle pairs attract rather than repel and clump together. A stability term $f^i_{\mathrm{divB},a}$ proportional to $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ is added to the momentum equation to counter this \cite{Borve2001}:
%the negative-definite part of the Maxwell stress can trigger a clumping instability---p
\begin{equation}
    f^i_{\mathrm{divB},a} = -\hat{B}^i_a \sum_b m_b \left[\frac{X_a^2}{\Omega_a m_a^2 k_a}(\mathbf{B}_a \cdot \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_a)) + \frac{X_b^2}{\Omega_b m_b^2 k_b}(\mathbf{B}_b \cdot \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_b))\right]\,,
    \label{eq:fdivb}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{B}^i_a = H(\beta_a)\, B^i_a$ is modulated by a switch $H$ that activates the correction only in the strong-field regime. SPH-EXA uses the Phantom switch \cite{Price2018}:
\begin{equation}
    H(\beta) = \begin{cases}
        1 & \beta < 2\,, \\
        \dfrac{10 - \beta}{8} & 2 \leq \beta < 10\,, \\
        0 & \beta \geq 10\,.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:beta_switch}
\end{equation}

This correction deliberately breaks exact momentum conservation; the error is proportional to local $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ and kept small by the divergence-cleaning scheme.


\subsection{Divergence cleaning}
\label{sec:divb_cleaning}

SPH-EXA controls the $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ constraint numerically through the constrained hyperbolic/parabolic cleaning scheme of Dedner et al. \cite{Dedner2002}, extended to SPH by Tricco \& Price \cite{TriccoPrice2012}. The scheme introduces a scalar field $\psi$ that couples to the induction equation and transports divergence errors away from their source while damping them according to a damped wave equation. 
% \subsubsection*{The coupled system}
The magnetic field receives an additional cleaning contribution
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{d\mathbf{B}}{dt}\right)_\psi = -\nabla \psi\,,
    \label{eq:cleaning_dBdt}
\end{equation}
and the auxiliary field $\psi$ evolves as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{\psi}{c_h}\right) = -c_h \, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} - \frac{\psi}{c_h \tau} - \frac{\psi}{2c_h}\, \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v}\,,
    \label{eq:psi_evolution}
\end{equation}
where $c_h$ is the cleaning speed and $\tau$ the damping time. %Combining \eqref{eq:cleaning_dBdt} and \eqref{eq:psi_evolution} in the limit of constant $c_h$ and $\tau$ yields a damped wave equation for $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ \cite{TriccoPrice2012}:
% \begin{equation}
%     \frac{\partial^2 (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B})}{\partial t^2} - c_h^2 \nabla^2(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) + \frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\partial(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B})}{\partial t} = 0\,.
%     \label{eq:divb_wave}
% \end{equation}
Divergence errors propagate outward at speed $c_h$ (hyperbolic cleaning) and decay exponentially with time constant $\tau$ (parabolic damping). SPH-EXA evolves the rescaled variable $\psi/c_h$ rather than $\psi$ directly, necessary for consistency when $c_h$ varies in space and time \cite{Tricco2016}.

% \subsubsection*{Cleaning parameters}

The cleaning speed $c_h$ is set to the fast magnetosonic speed $v_\mathrm{mhd}$ \eqref{eq:vmhd} so that its timestep constraint is already satisfied. The scheme can be made more aggressive by scaling $c_{h,a}$ with a factor $f_\mathrm{clean}>1$ at the cost of a smaller CFL timestep (see §\ref{sec:timeint}). Usually, keeping $f_\mathrm{clean}=1$ is desirable. %\cite{WissingShen2020}
% \begin{equation}
%     c_{h,a} = f_\mathrm{clean} \sqrt{c_{s,a}^2 + v_{A,a}^2}\,,
%     \label{eq:ch}
% \end{equation}
% with $f_\mathrm{clean} = 1$ (no over-cleaning; larger values improve divergence control at the cost of a smaller CFL time step). 
The damping time is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_a = \frac{h_a}{\sigma_c \, c_{h,a}}\,, \qquad \sigma_c = 1\,,
    \label{eq:tau_cleaning}
\end{equation}
coupling cleaning to local resolution and wave speed \cite{TriccoPrice2012}.

% \subsubsection*{Constrained discretization}
 
The SPH discretization of \eqref{eq:cleaning_dBdt}--\eqref{eq:psi_evolution} must be chosen carefully to avoid injecting energy. Tricco \& Price \cite{TriccoPrice2012} showed that the operators for $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ and $\nabla \psi$ must form a \emph{conjugate} pair: choosing the antisymmetric (difference) operator for one imposes the symmetric operator for the other. The cleaning dissipation rate is then negative-definite,
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{dE}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{clean} = -\sum_a m_a \frac{\psi_a^2}{\mu_0 \rho_a c_{h,a}^2 \tau_a}\,,
    \label{eq:cleaning_dissipation}
\end{equation}
i.e. cleaning can only remove magnetic energy, never inject it. SPH-EXA takes the difference operator for $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}$ in \eqref{eq:psi_evolution} and the symmetric one for $\nabla \psi$, adapted to the IAD gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{d\mathbf{B}_a}{dt}\right)_\psi = -\rho_a \sum_b m_b \left[\frac{\psi_a c_{h,a} X_a^2}{\Omega_a k_a m_a^2}\, \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_a) + \frac{\psi_b c_{h,b} X_b^2}{\Omega_b k_b m_b^2}\, \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_b)\right]\,,
    \label{eq:cleaning_grad_psi}
\end{equation}
with $\psi_a$ recovered from the evolved variable as $(\psi/c_h)_a c_{h,a}$. The removed energy is not reinjected as heat, since hyperbolic cleaning transports divergence errors away from where they originated \cite{TriccoPrice2012}.


\subsection{Artificial resistivity}
\label{sec:ar}

Physical discontinuities in the magnetic field cannot be captured by finite particles. Just like with artificial viscosity (AV, §\ref{sec:av}), energy at discontinuities gets trapped in sub-resolution gradients and produces high-frequency oscillations that corrupt the solution. Artificial resistivity (AR) acts as controlled diffusion of the magnetic field, spreading sharp gradients over a few smoothing lengths. Its contribution to the induction equation is \cite{Price2018, WissingShen2020}
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{d\mathbf{B}}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{diss} = \eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}\,,
    \label{eq:ar_continuum}
\end{equation}
with resistivity parameter $\eta = \tfrac{1}{2}\alpha_B\, v_{\mathrm{sig},B}\, |\mathbf{r}_{ab}|$, where $\alpha_B$ is a dimensionless coefficient and $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ the resistive signal speed \eqref{eq:vsigB}.

The Laplacian is estimated with the Brookshaw method \cite{Brookshaw1985}, using the first-derivative kernel and the field difference divided by the particle spacing. SPH-EXA replaces the kernel gradient by the IAD operator and uses the Phantom-style conjugate pair, evaluating both particles' kernels \cite{Price2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{d\mathbf{B}_a}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{diss} = \frac{\rho_a}{2} \sum_b m_b\, \alpha_{B,ab}\, v_{\mathrm{sig},B}\, \mathbf{B}_{ab}\, \mathcal{D}_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:ar_dB}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{du_a}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{diss} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} \sum_b m_b\, \alpha_{B,ab}\, v_{\mathrm{sig},B}\, B_{ab}^2\, \mathcal{D}_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:ar_du}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{B}_{ab} = \mathbf{B}_a - \mathbf{B}_b$, $\alpha_{B,ab} = \tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha_{B,a} + \alpha_{B,b})$, and
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}_{ab} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab} \cdot \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_a)}{\rho_a^2} + \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab} \cdot \boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}(h_b)}{\rho_b^2}\,.
    \label{eq:ar_kernel}
\end{equation}
The factor $|\mathbf{r}_{ab}|$ in $\eta$ cancels against the particle spacing in the Brookshaw denominator, so it does not appear explicitly. Equation \eqref{eq:ar_du} is the energy conjugate of \eqref{eq:ar_dB}: the magnetic energy lost is recovered exactly as heat. Setting $\rho_a = \rho_b$ and $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{ab} \to \nabla_a W_{ab}$ recovers the GDSPH form of Wissing \& Shen \cite{WissingShen2020}.

As with AV, we generally want to keep AR to a minimum in regions free from discontinuities and minimize the unphysical dissipation of magnetic energy. Analogously to AV, we can modulate per-particle $\alpha_B$ with heuristic switches, such as the one proposed by Tricco \& Price \cite{TriccoPrice2013}

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{B,a} = \min\!\left(\frac{h_a \|\nabla \mathbf{B}\|_a}{|\mathbf{B}_a|},\; 1\right)\,,
    \label{eq:tp_switch}
\end{equation}

where $\|\nabla \mathbf{B}\|$ is the Frobenius norm of the magnetic-field gradient tensor and serves as a steepness detector. This is large ($\sim 1$) near discontinuities and small in smooth regions.

This was later discarded by the authors themselves in \cite{Price2018}, where they found to optain better results using a constant value of $\alpha_B=1$, combined with a `self-switching' signal velocity

\begin{equation}
    v_{\mathrm{sig},B} = |\mathbf{v}_{ab} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab}|
    \label{eq:vsigB}
\end{equation}

instead of simply using the fast magnetosonic speed $v_\mathrm{mhd}$ \eqref{eq:vmhd}. This new $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ vanishes when there is no relative transverse motion between particles. In practice, transverse velocity differences are the only thing creating discontinuities in $\mathbf{B}$ that the induction equation cannot represent smoothly. Compression and shocks change together with density and are already smoothed by AV acting on $\rho$. 

However, $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ is a purely kinematic criterion. It decides \emph{when} the field is allowed to be dissipated, but knows nothing about \emph{what} is being dissipated: whether the pair difference $\mathbf{B}_{ab}$ is an unresolved jump that must be smoothed, or a perfectly resolved gradient that should be left alone. Any transverse velocity difference switches it on. This includes rigid rotation, where $|\mathbf{v}_{ab} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab}| = \Omega\,|\mathbf{r}_{ab}|$ for every pair, the small-scale velocity noise inherent to glass-like particle distributions, and, most importantly, the transverse motion of a resolved Alfvén wave, where $\mathbf{v}_\perp \neq 0$ \emph{is} the physics. In all of these cases $v_{\mathrm{sig},B} \sim |\delta v_\perp|$ everywhere, and a constant $\alpha_B = 1$ dissipates smooth field structure at full strength. The usual remedy is to lower the coefficient, and $\alpha_B = 0.5$ is a common compromise \cite{WissingShen2020}. This trades wave preservation against shock capturing globally and is what we use as the baseline scheme in §\ref{sec:numerical_tests}.

What is missing is spatial information about the field itself. The Tricco \& Price switch \eqref{eq:tp_switch} supplies it as a per-particle amplitude $\alpha_{B,a}$, which is a coarse answer: one scalar per particle cannot separate a resolved gradient from a discontinuity at the same location, and its threshold is a tuned heuristic. Slope-limited reconstruction offers a finer-grained alternative that acts directly on the pair difference, and which we adopt for the artificial resistivity in SPH-EXA.


\subsubsection{Slope-limited reconstruction for artificial resistivity (ARSLR)}
\label{sec:slr}

García-Senz \& Cabezón \cite{GarciaSenzCabezon2026} recently proposed slope-limited reconstruction (SLR) as a replacement for heuristic switches in artificial viscosity. Instead of scaling the amount of dissipation by a detector, the resolved linear part of the velocity field is subtracted from $\mathbf{v}_{ab}$ before it enters the viscous term, so that only the non-linear residual, the part SPH cannot represent, is dissipated. SPH-EXA implements the hydro variants of this scheme (AVSLR, AVSLRB2) for its AV and we refer to \cite{GarciaSenzCabezon2026} for their derivation and validation. Here we transfer the idea to the magnetic field.

The reconstruction replaces the raw difference $\mathbf{B}_{ab}$ in \eqref{eq:ar_dB} by
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}'_{ab} = \mathbf{B}_{ab} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi_{ab}\left(\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},a} + \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},b}\right)\mathbf{r}_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:slr_B}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B}}$ is the $3\times3$ Jacobian $\partial B^i/\partial x^j$, computed with the same IAD gradient operator as the velocity Jacobian \eqref{eq:vel_jacobian}. The term $\mathbf{J}_\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{r}_{ab}$ is the linear estimate of how much $\mathbf{B}$ changes between $a$ and $b$. In a smooth field this estimate accounts for almost the whole of $\mathbf{B}_{ab}$, so $\mathbf{B}'_{ab} \to 0$ and no dissipation acts, regardless of how large $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ is. Across a discontinuity or in incoherent noise the linear estimate fails, $\mathbf{B}'_{ab} \approx \mathbf{B}_{ab}$, and the dissipation acts at full strength with $\alpha_B = 1$. This is the scale separation that neither $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ nor a global $\alpha_B$ can provide: the scheme separates resolved from unresolved structure at the level of each pair, without a tuned threshold.

The limiter $\phi_{ab}$ protects the reconstruction where the linear estimate is unreliable. Following \cite{GarciaSenzCabezon2026, Frontiere2017} we use the van Leer form
\begin{equation}
    \phi_{ab} = \kappa_{ab}\, \max\!\left[0,\; \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{4 F_{ab}}{(1 + F_{ab})^2}\right)\right]\,, \qquad
    F_{ab} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{ab} \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},a}\, \mathbf{r}_{ab}}{\mathbf{r}_{ab} \cdot \mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},b}\, \mathbf{r}_{ab}}\,,
    \label{eq:vanleer}
\end{equation}
which compares the directional derivative of $\mathbf{B}$ along $\mathbf{r}_{ab}$ as seen from both particles. If the two estimates agree ($F_{ab} \approx 1$) the field is locally linear and the full correction is applied. If they disagree in magnitude or sign, as at a discontinuity, $\phi_{ab} \to 0$ and the raw difference is recovered. The expression is symmetric under $F_{ab} \to 1/F_{ab}$, so a single $\phi_{ab}$ serves both particles. The close-pair guard $\kappa_{ab}$ \cite{Frontiere2017} switches the reconstruction off smoothly for anomalously close pairs, $\kappa_{ab} = \exp[-((q_{ab} - q_\mathrm{crit})/q_\mathrm{fold})^2]$ for $q_{ab} < q_\mathrm{crit}$ and $1$ otherwise, with $q_{ab} = \min(|\mathbf{r}_{ab}|/h_a, |\mathbf{r}_{ab}|/h_b)$, $q_\mathrm{crit} = (32\pi/3n_b)^{1/3}$ the mean inter-particle spacing in units of $h$, and $q_\mathrm{fold} = 0.2$.

The heating term \eqref{eq:ar_du} is evaluated with $\mathbf{B}_{ab} \cdot \mathbf{B}'_{ab}$ in place of $B_{ab}^2$. This keeps it the exact energy conjugate of the reconstructed induction term, so the magnetic energy removed by \eqref{eq:slr_B} still reappears entirely as heat.


\subsubsection*{Steepness modulation (SLRB, SLRB2)}

In the hydro scheme, García-Senz \& Cabezón additionally weight the reconstruction with the Balsara factor \eqref{eq:balsara}, so that the linear part is not subtracted at shocks ($\mathcal{B}_a \to 1$) and full dissipation is guaranteed there. The Balsara factor has no clean magnetic analogue: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} \approx 0$ is a constraint rather than a flow feature, and the discontinuities that AR must treat (current sheets, rotational discontinuities) are non-compressive, so a velocity-based detector would read zero exactly where resistivity is needed. We instead use the Tricco \& Price steepness measure \eqref{eq:tp_switch} as a per-particle modulator,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{m}_a = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{h_a\, \|\nabla \mathbf{B}\|_a}{|\mathbf{B}_a|}\right)\,,
    \label{eq:slr_modulator}
\end{equation}
which is of order unity where the field varies over less than a smoothing length and small in smooth regions. It enters the reconstruction as a weight on each Jacobian term,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}'_{ab} = \mathbf{B}_{ab} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi_{ab}\left[(1 - \mathrm{m}_a^p)\,\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},a} + (1 - \mathrm{m}_b^p)\,\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{B},b}\right]\mathbf{r}_{ab}\,,
    \label{eq:slr_B_mod}
\end{equation}
with $p = 1$ (SLRB) or $p = 2$ (SLRB2). The latter mirrors the $(1 - \mathcal{B}_a^2)$ weighting of the hydro AVSLRB2 scheme. Near a steep feature the weight vanishes, the reconstruction is suppressed and the raw difference is dissipated. In smooth regions the weight is close to one and the full SLR correction applies. Finally, a pair-averaged floor
\begin{equation}
    L_{ab} = \max\!\left(F_\mathrm{floor},\; \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{m}_a + \mathrm{m}_b)\right)\,, \qquad
    \eta_\mathrm{eff} = L_{ab}\, \alpha_B\, v_{\mathrm{sig},B}\,,
    \label{eq:Lij}
\end{equation}
can scale the whole dissipation coefficient in the same way as the linear AV term in \cite{GarciaSenzCabezon2026}. With the default $F_\mathrm{floor} = 1$ this factor is inactive and $L_{ab} \equiv 1$.

The combination of $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ with ARSLR is not found in the literature and its behaviour is the subject of the tests in §\ref{sec:numerical_tests}. The two mechanisms are complementary: $v_{\mathrm{sig},B}$ determines when dissipation may act, the reconstruction determines where a genuinely unresolved field structure exists to be dissipated. The hope is that this removes the need for the global compromise of a reduced $\alpha_B$, dissipating incoherent noise and true discontinuities at full strength while preserving resolved structure such as waves.


\subsection{Artificial Conductivity}
\label{sec:ac}

At contact discontinuities (temperature jumps at constant pressure and density), SPH's Lagrangian nature can produce small-scale pressure blips because particles with different internal energies are in close proximity but the kernel-smoothed pressure field is not exactly balanced. Artificial conductivity provides localized thermal diffusion to smooth this artifact \cite{Price2018}:
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{du_a}{dt}\right)_\mathrm{cond} = \alpha_u \sum_b m_b \frac{v^\mathrm{sig}_u \, (u_a - u_b)}{\bar{\rho}_{ab}}\; (\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab} \cdot \nabla_a W_{ab})\,,
    \label{eq:ac}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_u$ is the conductivity coefficient and $v^\mathrm{sig}_u = |\mathbf{v}_{ab} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ab}|$ is the approach-velocity signal speed (with gravity) \cite{Rosswog2009}. The term is only active for converging particles. In MHD specifically, it smooths spurious temperature spikes near fast and slow shocks.

\subsection{Time Integration}
\label{sec:timeint}

The magnetic-field components and the cleaning field $\psi/c_h$ are advanced in time using the same Adams--Bashforth second-order (AB2) scheme as the hydro equations
\begin{equation}
    q^{n+1} = q^n + \dot{q}^n \, \Delta t + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\dot{q}^n - \dot{q}^{n-1}}{\Delta t_{n-1}}\, |\Delta t|\, \Delta t\,,
    \label{eq:ab2}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{q}^n$ denotes the time derivative at the current step and $\dot{q}^{n-1}$ that at the previous step. 

The time step is constrained by the CFL condition
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t = K_\mathrm{cour} \min_a \frac{h_a}{\max(v_{\mathrm{sig},a},\; v_{\mathrm{mhd},a})}\,,
    \label{eq:cfl}
\end{equation}
where $v_\mathrm{sig}$ is the pairwise signal velocity from AV and $v_\mathrm{mhd}$ the local fast magnetosonic speed \eqref{eq:vmhd}.
